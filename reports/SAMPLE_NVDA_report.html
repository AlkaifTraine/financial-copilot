Business Overview

NVIDIA generates $215.9B revenue primarily from Compute & Networking and Graphics segments, serving data center, gaming, and automotive markets globally in FY2026.

NVIDIA's revenue for fiscal year 2026 reached $215.9 billion, driven predominantly by its Compute & Networking segment which accounted for $193.5 billion, while the Graphics segment contributed $22.5 billion. The company’s business model centers on selling advanced semiconductor products and systems primarily for accelerated computing and AI applications, with a strong emphasis on data center computing and networking solutions. This shift towards AI and accelerated computing platforms underpins the substantial year-over-year growth in Compute & Networking revenue, which increased by 66% from $116.2 billion in FY2025 to $193.5 billion in FY2026, reflecting demand for the Blackwell computing platform and NVLink fabric systems [2][7].

NVIDIA’s revenue by end market further illustrates its focus on data center technologies, which generated $193.7 billion or approximately 90% of total revenue in FY2026. Within this, Compute revenue was $162.4 billion and Networking $31.4 billion, both showing significant growth driven by hyperscalers and diversified AI cloud customers. Gaming revenue, supported by the Blackwell architecture, contributed $16 billion, up 41% year-over-year, while Professional Visualization and Automotive markets also saw double-digit growth, reflecting NVIDIA’s expanding footprint in self-driving platforms and workstation solutions [3][8].

Geographically, NVIDIA’s revenue is concentrated in the United States, which accounted for $149.6 billion or 69% of total revenue in FY2026, followed by Taiwan and China with $42.3 billion and $19.7 billion respectively. This geographic distribution aligns with the location of direct customers such as AI model makers, cloud service providers, and OEMs, with a significant portion of data center revenue linked to U.S.-based end customers despite some sales being booked through Taiwan-headquartered customers [1][4].

NVIDIA’s revenue concentration is notable, with two direct customers representing 36% of total revenue in FY2026, primarily within the Compute & Networking segment. The company sells directly to a limited number of large customers including AI clouds, system integrators, and OEMs, who may then serve indirect customers such as enterprises and public sector entities. This customer concentration underscores NVIDIA’s strategic positioning in high-performance computing and AI infrastructure markets, where it leverages its proprietary semiconductor technologies and system-level solutions to capture value [4][7].

Financial Performance

NVIDIA's FY2026 revenue surged 65% driven by AI and accelerated computing, with margin compression and higher expenses impacting profitability dynamics.

NVIDIA reported a 65% increase in revenue to $215.9 billion for fiscal year 2026, primarily fueled by a 68% rise in Data Center revenue due to accelerated computing and AI platform shifts, alongside a 41% growth in Gaming revenue driven by strong Blackwell demand. Professional Visualization and Automotive revenues also expanded by 70% and 39%, respectively, reflecting robust demand for new product launches and self-driving platform adoption. This top-line growth underscores NVIDIA's successful capitalizing on major technological trends and product cycles [1][3].

Despite the substantial revenue growth, gross margin declined by 3.9 percentage points to 71.1%, attributed to the transition from Hopper HGX systems to Blackwell full-scale datacenter solutions and a significant $4.5 billion charge related to H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations. Cost of revenue increased to $62.5 billion, representing 28.9% of revenue, up from 25.0% in the prior year, reflecting these structural and inventory-related cost pressures [1][2][4].

Operating expenses rose 41% to $23.1 billion, driven by higher compensation and benefits costs linked to employee growth, as well as increased compute and infrastructure expenses. Research and development expenses increased 43% to $18.5 billion, while sales, general and administrative expenses grew 31% to $4.6 billion, reflecting NVIDIA's investments to support its expanding business and innovation pipeline [1][2][5].

Operating income increased 60% to $130.4 billion, with operating margin slightly contracting to 60.4% from 62.4%. Net income rose 65% to $120.1 billion, and diluted EPS increased 67% to $4.90, supported by strong revenue growth and operational leverage despite margin pressures. Cash flow from operations improved significantly to $102.7 billion, up from $64.1 billion, driven by higher net income and working capital changes, enabling increased investments and shareholder returns [1][2][7][8].

Growth Drivers

NVIDIA's revenue growth is driven by accelerated computing, AI platform shifts, and expanding product architectures amid supply chain complexities.

NVIDIA's fiscal year 2026 revenue surged 65% year-over-year to $215.9 billion, propelled primarily by a 68% increase in Data Center revenue and a 41% rise in Gaming revenue, with the latter driven by strong demand for the Blackwell architecture. Professional Visualization and Automotive segments also posted significant growth of 70% and 39%, respectively, reflecting robust adoption of new platforms such as DGX Spark and self-driving technologies. This growth is underpinned by major platform shifts towards accelerated computing and AI, which management identifies as core revenue drivers already visible in results [2][6][7].

The company is executing a strategy of increasing product and architecture cadence, particularly in Data Center solutions, aiming to introduce new computing solutions annually and broaden its product portfolio, including the upcoming Rubin platform expected to ship in the second half of fiscal 2027. This accelerated launch cadence is designed to capture diverse AI opportunities but introduces complexity in supply chain management, product qualification, and revenue timing, as customers may delay adoption pending infrastructure readiness or inventory adjustments. These factors contribute to revenue volatility and margin pressure, exemplified by recent inventory provisions related to low-yielding Blackwell material and excess H20 inventory charges totaling $4.5 billion [1][3][8].

NVIDIA is also expanding its U.S.-based manufacturing capabilities and investing in specialized equipment to support domestic production, aiming to mitigate supply constraints and enhance supply chain resilience amid geopolitical and logistical risks. However, scaling production remains contingent on the domestic manufacturing ecosystem's capacity, with potential delays posing risks to meeting demand. The company acknowledges the challenges of managing multiple architectures concurrently across Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive segments, which complicates demand forecasting and supply planning, potentially impacting gross margins and financial results [1][3][5][8].

Looking forward, NVIDIA's growth drivers include continued adoption of AI and accelerated computing platforms, expansion of product architectures, and strategic investments in early-stage AI startups and infrastructure funds to support ecosystem development. However, risks remain from supply-demand mismatches, evolving customer requirements, and competitive pressures, which could affect revenue timing and magnitude. Management's commentary underscores a balance between leveraging rapid innovation cycles and managing operational complexities inherent in a fast-evolving AI-driven market [2][5][8].

Competitive Position

NVIDIA faces intense competition from established semiconductor firms and cloud providers, with export controls and supply chain constraints challenging its market position.

NVIDIA competes primarily with major semiconductor companies such as AMD, Intel, Huawei, and Broadcom, as well as large cloud service providers including Amazon, Microsoft, and Alibaba, who develop internal hardware and software solutions incorporating AI and accelerated computing functionalities. The competitive landscape spans discrete and integrated GPUs, CPUs, DPUs, SoCs, and networking products, with rivals often possessing greater financial, manufacturing, and marketing resources, longer operating histories, and more extensive IP portfolios, enabling them to adapt more rapidly to technological changes and customer demands [1][2][6].

The company’s competitive position is protected by its broad product portfolio, software support, manufacturing partnerships with leading foundries like TSMC and Samsung, and its ability to deliver consistent volumes at acceptable quality and competitive prices. However, NVIDIA acknowledges increasing competition from lower-priced or higher-performing products and the risk of new entrants or alliances capturing significant market share [1][6].

Geopolitical and regulatory factors notably impair NVIDIA’s competitive stance, particularly its effective exclusion from China’s data center market due to complex U.S. and Chinese export controls. This foreclosure has allowed competitors to expand their developer and customer ecosystems globally, materially impacting NVIDIA’s business and financial condition. Additionally, evolving U.S. export controls, such as the rescinded AI Diffusion IFR and the potential implications of the GAIN AI Act, introduce uncertainty and may impose burdensome licensing requirements that could further constrain NVIDIA’s market access and operations [3][4][5].

Supply chain concentration in Asia and long manufacturing lead times, sometimes exceeding 12 months, expose NVIDIA to risks of supply-demand mismatches and increased costs from premiums and deposits to secure capacity. While efforts to diversify manufacturing into the U.S. and Latin America are underway, these constraints, combined with macroeconomic factors like tariffs and inflation, may adversely affect NVIDIA’s cost structure and competitive pricing ability [2][5][6][7][8].

Key Risks

NVIDIA faces material risks from customer concentration, export controls, supply chain dependencies, and regulatory scrutiny impacting revenue and operations.

NVIDIA's revenue concentration risk is significant, with two direct customers accounting for 36% of total revenue in fiscal 2026, primarily in the Compute & Networking segment. This concentration exposes NVIDIA to substantial operational risk if these customers reduce purchases, alter buying patterns, or if geopolitical trade restrictions prevent sales, potentially harming financial results and cash flow timing due to the purchase order nature of sales and credit limitations of channel partners [1].

Export controls and geopolitical tensions critically impair NVIDIA's competitive positioning and market access, especially in China, where U.S. restrictions limit product offerings and the Chinese government actively promotes domestic competitors. These controls complicate licensing, increase compliance costs, and may force customers to seek alternatives, thereby eroding NVIDIA's market share and revenue in key segments such as Gaming and Data Center [2][8].

Supply chain risks are heightened by NVIDIA's reliance on third-party manufacturing concentrated in Asia, particularly Taiwan and South Korea. Disruptions or new export restrictions affecting these regions could materially impact supply continuity and production costs, thereby reducing gross margins and operational flexibility. Additionally, rising costs across wafers, components, logistics, and labor have historically pressured margins and remain a persistent threat [3][4][8].

Regulatory and legal risks are multifaceted, encompassing complex global compliance obligations including antitrust investigations, data privacy, cybersecurity, and AI governance. Increased regulatory scrutiny, exemplified by ongoing inquiries from multiple jurisdictions into NVIDIA's market conduct and competitive practices, may impose substantial compliance costs, operational constraints, and reputational damage, potentially affecting long-term strategic initiatives and market expansion [5][6].
