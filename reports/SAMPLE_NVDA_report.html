Business Overview

NVIDIA generates revenue primarily from Compute & Networking and Graphics segments, serving direct and indirect customers in AI, gaming, and data centers.

NVIDIA's business is organized into two operating segments: Compute & Networking and Graphics. In fiscal year 2026, Compute & Networking accounted for $193.5 billion of revenue, representing approximately 90% of total revenue, while Graphics contributed $22.5 billion, about 10% of total revenue. Operating income was $130.1 billion for Compute & Networking and $9.2 billion for Graphics, reflecting the dominant profitability of the Compute & Networking segment driven by accelerated computing and AI platform shifts [1].

The Compute & Networking segment's growth was propelled by a 59% increase in Data Center computing revenue, fueled by demand for the Blackwell computing platform, and a 142% rise in Data Center networking revenue due to NVLink fabric and Ethernet/InfiniBand platform expansion. Graphics revenue grew 41% year-over-year, driven by strong Blackwell GPU demand in gaming and professional visualization markets [SOURCE 3, SOURCE 8].

NVIDIA's customer base includes direct customers such as add-in board manufacturers, distributors, ODMs, OEMs, cloud service providers, AI model makers, and system integrators, as well as indirect customers who purchase through these direct customers. Revenue concentration is significant, with one direct customer accounting for 22% and another for 14% of total revenue in fiscal 2026, primarily within Compute & Networking. Indirect customers include hyperscale cloud providers and enterprises, some contributing over 10% of revenue individually [SOURCE 5, SOURCE 8].

Geographically, 69% of NVIDIA's revenue in fiscal 2026 was from customers headquartered in the United States, with Taiwan and China contributing 20% and 9%, respectively. Notably, a substantial portion of Taiwan-headquartered Data Center revenue is attributed to end customers in the U.S. and Europe. Export restrictions on certain high-performance H20 products to China have led to a $4.5 billion inventory charge, impacting gross margins and future revenue prospects in that market [SOURCE 2, SOURCE 4, SOURCE 7].

Financial Performance

NVIDIA's FY2026 revenue surged 65% driven by AI and accelerated computing, while margins contracted due to product mix and inventory charges.

NVIDIA reported fiscal year 2026 revenue of $215.9 billion, a 65% increase from $130.5 billion in the prior year, primarily propelled by Data Center revenue growth of 68%, fueled by accelerated computing and AI platform shifts. Gaming revenue rose 41% on strong Blackwell demand, though supply constraints are expected to impact future quarters. Professional Visualization and Automotive segments also saw robust growth of 70% and 39%, respectively, driven by Blackwell adoption and self-driving platform expansion [1][2].

Gross margin declined by 3.9 percentage points to 71.1% from 75.0% in FY2025, reflecting a strategic transition from Hopper HGX systems to Blackwell full-scale datacenter solutions. This margin contraction was further exacerbated by a $4.5 billion charge related to H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations. Despite this, gross margin improved sequentially in Q3 FY2026 due to Blackwell ramp and better cost structure [2][6][7].

Operating expenses increased 41% to $23.1 billion, driven by higher compensation and benefits from employee growth, as well as increased compute and infrastructure costs. Research and development expenses rose 43% to $18.5 billion, while sales, general and administrative expenses grew 31% to $4.6 billion, reflecting investments in new product development and scaling operations [1][4][6][7].

Operating income rose 60% to $130.4 billion, supported by strong revenue growth despite margin pressures. Net income increased 65% to $120.1 billion, with diluted net income per share up 67% to $4.90. Cash flow from operations surged 60% to $102.7 billion, enabling significant investment in property and equipment and returning capital through financing activities [1][5][8].

Growth Drivers

NVIDIA's revenue growth is driven by AI platform adoption, architectural transitions, and expanding automotive and industrial AI markets, with supply constraints as a near-term risk.

NVIDIA's fiscal 2026 revenue surged 65% to $215.9 billion, propelled by accelerated computing and AI platform shifts, notably the Blackwell architecture driving 41% gaming revenue growth and 70% professional visualization growth. Data Center revenue increased 68%, reflecting strong demand for Blackwell full-scale datacenter solutions despite a gross margin decline due to transition costs and inventory charges. Automotive revenue rose 39%, fueled by adoption of self-driving platforms, with new AI models like Alpamayo enabling vehicles capable of reasoning and action, exemplified by the imminent Mercedes-Benz CLA deployment. These drivers are already visible in results, underscoring the centrality of AI and platform innovation to NVIDIA's current financial performance [1][2].

Looking forward, management emphasizes exponential growth in AI compute demand across training and inference, with Blackwell and upcoming Vera Rubin architectures expected to extend NVIDIA's leadership in cost-efficient inference and performance. The AI ecosystem's rapid scaling, including foundation model makers and startups across industries and geographies, underpins sustained demand. Enterprise adoption of AI agents and industrial physical AI applications, supported by partnerships with Dassault Systèmes, Siemens, and Synopsys, signal prospective growth avenues beyond current revenue streams. Robotics and autonomous machine deployments with partners like Boston Dynamics and Caterpillar further diversify growth drivers [2][4][5].

However, NVIDIA faces risks from complex and frequent architectural transitions across Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive segments. These transitions can induce revenue volatility due to inventory management challenges, customer adoption delays, and potential quality or production issues, as evidenced by prior Blackwell inventory provisions impacting margins. Additionally, supply constraints, particularly for advanced architectures, are expected to persist into fiscal 2027, although NVIDIA's strategic inventory build and supply chain scale aim to mitigate these headwinds. The company’s ability to ramp U.S.-based manufacturing and manage product complexity will be critical to sustaining growth momentum [1][6][7].

Management expresses confidence in growth visibility into calendar 2027, supported by increased purchase commitments reflecting demand beyond typical horizons. Despite concerns about cloud customers' capital expenditure growth amid compressed cash flows, NVIDIA believes its roadmap and platform leadership will enable growth even if cloud CapEx growth slows, indicating resilience in its addressable market and customer engagement [2][8].

  • Fiscal 2026 revenue grew 65% to $215.9 billion, with Data Center up 68% and Gaming up 41% driven by Blackwell demand [1].
  • Automotive revenue increased 39%, supported by self-driving platform adoption and new AI models like Alpamayo in Mercedes-Benz vehicles [1][2].
  • Blackwell and Vera Rubin architectures are central to future AI compute demand growth, delivering order-of-magnitude improvements in inference cost and performance [3][5].
  • Partnerships with Dassault Systèmes, Siemens, and Synopsys aim to accelerate industrial AI adoption via NVIDIA AI infrastructure and digital twins [2].
  • Supply constraints and architectural transitions pose risks to revenue timing and margins, with prior inventory charges of $4.5 billion related to H20 excess inventory [1][6][7].
  • NVIDIA has secured inventory and capacity for demand beyond several quarters, reflecting confidence in growth visibility into calendar 2027 despite macroeconomic uncertainties [2][8].

Competitive Position

NVIDIA faces intense competition from established semiconductor firms and cloud providers, with export controls and supply constraints challenging its market position.

NVIDIA competes primarily with major semiconductor companies such as AMD, Intel, Broadcom, and Qualcomm, as well as large cloud service providers including Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet, who develop internal AI and accelerated computing solutions. These competitors often possess greater manufacturing resources, longer operating histories, and more extensive IP portfolios, enabling them to adapt more rapidly to technological shifts and customer demands, thereby intensifying competitive pressure on NVIDIA’s market share and pricing power [2][1].

The company’s fabless manufacturing model relies heavily on third-party foundries like TSMC and Samsung, exposing NVIDIA to supply chain risks including long lead times exceeding 12 months and capacity constraints. To mitigate this, NVIDIA has engaged in long-term supply agreements and paid premiums to secure capacity, which increases product costs and may limit responsiveness to demand fluctuations. These supply challenges, coupled with competitors’ in-house fabrication capabilities, constrain NVIDIA’s ability to scale and maintain competitive pricing [5][1][7].

Geopolitical factors further erode NVIDIA’s competitive position, particularly U.S. export controls that effectively bar the company from China’s data center market. This exclusion has enabled competitors to expand their ecosystems and customer bases in China, diminishing NVIDIA’s global market opportunities. The complex and evolving licensing regime imposes operational burdens and may delay or restrict product availability, benefiting competitors and encouraging customers to seek alternative suppliers or develop internal solutions [3][4].

Despite these challenges, NVIDIA’s competitive strengths include its broad product portfolio, software ecosystem, and partnerships, but the company acknowledges that intensified competition, supply constraints, and regulatory restrictions could materially reduce demand for its products and adversely impact financial results [1][2][5].

  • Key competitors include AMD, Intel, Broadcom, Qualcomm, and cloud providers like Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet [2].
  • Manufacturing lead times can exceed 12 months, with premiums paid to secure foundry capacity, increasing costs [1][7].
  • Export controls have effectively foreclosed NVIDIA from China’s data center market, benefiting competitors [3][4].
  • Inventory provisions related to excess or obsolete products negatively impacted gross margin by 2.6% in fiscal 2026 [7].
  • NVIDIA’s supply chain is concentrated in Asia but is expanding to the U.S. and Latin America to build resilience [5].
  • Competitive factors include performance, product breadth, software support, pricing, and manufacturing capabilities [5].

Key Risks

NVIDIA faces material risks from customer concentration, export controls, supply chain vulnerabilities, and regulatory scrutiny impacting revenue and operations.

NVIDIA's revenue concentration is a significant risk, with two direct customers accounting for 36% of total revenue in fiscal 2026, primarily within the Compute & Networking segment. This concentration exposes NVIDIA to substantial operational and financial risk if these customers reduce purchases, alter buying patterns, or if trade restrictions prevent sales, given that most sales are on a cancellable purchase order basis and indirect customers often purchase through multiple intermediaries, complicating revenue predictability and collection [1].

Export controls and geopolitical tensions critically impair NVIDIA's competitive position and market access, particularly in China, where U.S. export restrictions limit product offerings and the Chinese government actively encourages purchases from domestic competitors while imposing gaming and data center regulations that could materially reduce Gaming and Data Center revenues. These controls also complicate technical support and licensing, potentially benefiting competitors and disrupting customer relationships in key international markets [2][6].

Supply chain risks are heightened by NVIDIA's reliance on Asian manufacturing partners, primarily in Taiwan and South Korea, with increased costs for wafers, components, and logistics negatively impacting gross margins. The company’s efforts to expand U.S.-based manufacturing aim to enhance supply chain resiliency but depend on domestic ecosystem capacity and timely scale-up. Macroeconomic factors such as tariffs, inflation, and capital market volatility further exacerbate supply chain complexity and cost pressures, while product pricing remains relatively inflexible to short-term cost fluctuations [3][4][7].

Regulatory and legal risks are extensive and evolving, encompassing export restrictions, antitrust inquiries, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, and compliance with diverse international laws. NVIDIA faces ongoing investigations by competition authorities in multiple jurisdictions, which are costly and may disrupt business relationships. Additionally, cybersecurity threats, including sophisticated ransomware and AI-enabled attacks, pose operational risks that could impair revenue and reputation. Corporate sustainability expectations and related regulatory requirements also present potential cost and reputational risks [5][8].

  • 36% of fiscal 2026 revenue derived from two direct customers, increasing dependency risk [1].
  • 31% of revenue generated outside the U.S., with China market access constrained by export controls and government policies [5][6].
  • Supply chain costs for wafers, components, and logistics have increased, pressuring gross margins [4].
  • 3.7% employee turnover rate in fiscal 2026, reflecting workforce stability amid operational risks [7].
  • Ongoing antitrust investigations by EU, U.S., UK, China, and South Korea regulators impose significant compliance costs and operational uncertainty [8].
  • Cybersecurity threats, including AI-enabled attacks, increasingly jeopardize operational continuity and data security [5].

Outlook

NVIDIA's capital allocation emphasizes aggressive share repurchases and consistent dividends, with substantial repurchase authorizations and flexible execution plans.

NVIDIA's management has demonstrated a robust commitment to returning capital to shareholders through an aggressive share repurchase program and steady dividend payments. In fiscal year 2026, the company repurchased 282 million shares for $40.4 billion, reflecting a significant acceleration compared to prior periods. This activity was supported by multiple authorizations, including a $60.0 billion additional repurchase authorization approved on August 26, 2025, which increased total available repurchase capacity to $71.2 billion by that date and remained at $62.2 billion as of October 26, 2025. The repurchases are executed flexibly across open market transactions, Rule 10b5-1 trading plans, and structured agreements, allowing management to optimize timing and pricing subject to market conditions and operational needs [1][3][5][8].

Dividend payments have been consistent and substantial, totaling $974 million in fiscal year 2026, with quarterly dividends of approximately $244 million each quarter. However, future dividends remain subject to the Board's discretion, emphasizing a cautious approach to balancing shareholder returns with operational liquidity [1][2][3][4][5][6][7].

Management explicitly states that share repurchases aim to offset dilution from employee equity compensation while maintaining adequate liquidity for operations and other investment opportunities. The company also incorporates the 1% excise tax on certain repurchases mandated by the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 into repurchase costs, though this tax has not materially impacted repurchase expenses to date. The share repurchase program may be suspended at any time at management's discretion, underscoring a prudent and adaptive capital allocation strategy [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].

  • As of October 26, 2025, $62.2 billion remained authorized for repurchase [5].
